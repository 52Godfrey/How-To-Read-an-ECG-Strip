01 · The electrical system

Every wave is one step in the wiring

An ECG doesn't show the heart squeezing. It shows electricity moving through it. Each wave lines up with one part of the conduction system, so when a wave looks wrong, you know which part of the wiring has the problem.

fires firstSinoatrial (SA) nodeThe natural pacemaker in the right atrium. Sets the rate at 60 to 100.
P waveAtria depolarizeSignal spreads across both atria and they contract.
PR segmentAtrioventricular (AV) nodeA built-in pause so the ventricles can fill. Heart blocks happen here.
QRS complexBundle of His → Purkinje fibersSignal races down the bundle branches and the ventricles contract.
T waveVentricles repolarizeThe ventricles reset and get ready for the next beat.

Backup pacemakers

If a higher site fails, the next one down takes over at its own, slower rate. The rate tells you where the beat is coming from.

SiteRateQRS
Sinoatrial node60–100Narrow, with a P wave
Atrioventricular junction40–60Narrow, P wave missing or upside down
Ventricles20–40Wide and bizarre

Narrow vs wide QRS

A narrow QRS (under 0.12 seconds) means the beat traveled the normal highway down the bundle branches, so it started above the ventricles.

A wide QRS means the beat started in the ventricles or took a detour, cell to cell. Wide and fast is the pattern to fear: think ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise.

03 · The paper and the rate

Measuring on the grid

Standard ECG paper runs at 25 millimeters per second. Every box is a fixed amount of time, so you measure intervals by counting boxes.

small box

0.04 s

1 millimeter wide. Vertically, 0.1 millivolt.

large box

0.20 s

5 small boxes. 5 large boxes = 1 second.

6-second strip

30 large

Monitor strips often print hash marks every 3 seconds.

Three ways to calculate heart rate

6-second method

Count the QRS complexes in 6 seconds and multiply by 10.

Best for irregular rhythms like atrial fibrillation. Fast, but an estimate.

Sequence method

Find an R wave on a heavy line. Count the next heavy lines: 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50. Where the next R lands is the rate.

Quick at the bedside. Regular rhythms only.

1500 method

Count small boxes between two R waves. Rate = 1500 ÷ boxes.

Most precise. Example: 20 boxes → 75 per minute. Regular rhythms only.

Try it: in the rhythm library below, click two R waves on any strip and the calipers will tell you the time between them, the number of small boxes, and the rate.

04 · The systematic method

Read every strip in the same order

When you panic, you skip steps. A fixed order keeps you from jumping to a diagnosis. Always look at the patient first.

  1. Check the patient. Pulse, responsiveness, BP. A scary strip on a talking patient may be artifact. A "normal" strip on a pulseless patient is pulseless electrical activity.
  2. Rate. Under 60 is bradycardia, over 100 is tachycardia. Check both atrial (P) and ventricular (QRS) rates if they differ.
  3. Rhythm. Are the R-to-R distances even? Regular, regularly irregular (a pattern), or irregularly irregular (no pattern at all)?
  4. P waves. Present? Upright? One before every QRS? Same shape every time?
  5. PR interval. 0.12–0.20 seconds? The same every beat, or changing?
  6. QRS width. Narrow (under 0.12) or wide?
  7. ST segment, T waves, QT. Raised, dipped, peaked, flat, inverted, long?
  8. Name it, then decide. Name the rhythm, then ask: is the patient stable or unstable? That answer decides what you do next.
05 · Worked example

Reading a strip, one question at a time

This is the same strip style as your worksheet. Answer the questions in order and the interpretation falls out at the end. Every number below is counted right off the grid.

Zoomed in so you can count: each small box here is 0.04 seconds, each large box (5 small) is 0.20 seconds.

Rate?
  1. Find an R wave (the tall spike), ideally one sitting on a grid line.
  2. Count the small boxes to the next R wave. Here: 35 small boxes.
  3. Divide 1500 by that number: 1500 ÷ 35 ≈ 43.

Why 1500? The paper moves 1500 small boxes every minute (25 per second × 60 seconds). Dividing by the boxes in one beat tells you how many beats fit in a minute.

Faster with large boxes: 35 small boxes = 7 large boxes, and 300 ÷ 7 ≈ 43. Same answer.

About 43 per minute. Under 60, so bradycardia.
Regular?
Measure the next R-to-R gap the same way. If every gap is the same (within about 1 to 3 small boxes), it's regular. Here both gaps are 35 small boxes.
Yes, regular.
P wave for every QRS?
Look just in front of each tall spike for a small rounded bump. Check that every QRS has one, that they're upright and the same shape, and that there are no extra P waves standing alone.
Yes, one P before every QRS.
PR interval under 0.20?
Count from where the P wave starts to where the QRS starts. Here: 4 small boxes, so 4 × 0.04 = 0.16 seconds. Check that it's the same on every beat.
Yes, 0.16 seconds, normal and constant.
Narrow QRS?
Count the width of the spike from where it leaves the baseline to where it returns. Here: 2 small boxes, so 0.08 seconds. Under 3 boxes (0.12 seconds) is narrow.
Yes, narrow.
Interpret the rhythm

Counting tips

  • Count from the peak of one R wave to the peak of the next, not from the base.
  • If an R wave lands between lines, estimate the partial box. Being off by one box only shifts the rate by a beat or two at slow rates.
  • Use the 1500 method only when the rhythm is regular. For an irregular rhythm like atrial fibrillation, count the QRS complexes in 6 seconds and multiply by 10.
  • The same rule names all three sinus rhythms: under 60 is sinus bradycardia, 60 to 100 is normal sinus rhythm, over 100 is sinus tachycardia.

07 · Leads

Where the stickers go, and what each lead sees

Each lead is a camera angle on the heart. A bedside monitor uses 3 or 5 electrodes and shows one or two angles. A 12-lead uses 10 electrodes to build 12 views, which is how you find where a heart attack is.

5-lead monitor (United States colors)

  • White on the right: right upper chest, below the clavicle
  • Black: left upper chest. "Smoke over fire": black sits above red.
  • Red: left lower chest or abdomen
  • Green: right lower chest or abdomen. "Snow over grass": white sits above green.
  • Brown: chest, usually the V1 position

Lead II is the default monitoring lead because it shows the clearest P waves.

12-lead chest electrodes

  • V1: 4th intercostal space, right of the sternum
  • V2: 4th intercostal space, left of the sternum
  • V3: halfway between V2 and V4
  • V4: 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line
  • V5: same level as V4, anterior axillary line
  • V6: same level as V4, midaxillary line

Plus four limb electrodes. Have the patient lie still, flat or semi-Fowler's, and get it done within 10 minutes of chest pain.

08 · Ischemia and infarction

Spotting a heart attack

Ischemia means heart muscle isn't getting enough oxygen. Infarction means it's dying. The ST segment and T wave tell you which, and the leads tell you where.

What you see

What it means and what you do

How a STEMI evolves

  1. Minutes: tall, broad "hyperacute" T waves
  2. Minutes to hours: ST elevation
  3. Hours to days: Q waves form (dead muscle)
  4. Days to weeks: T waves invert, ST returns to baseline

STEMI means ST-elevation myocardial infarction: at least 1 millimeter of ST elevation in 2 leads that look at the same wall. Leads on the opposite side often show "reciprocal" ST depression.

Rules that trip students up

  • A normal ECG does not rule out a heart attack. Troponin does, usually repeated over several hours.
  • NSTEMI (non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction) has ST depression or T inversion plus a rising troponin.
  • Unstable angina has symptoms but a normal troponin.
  • For a STEMI, time is muscle: the goal is the cardiac catheterization lab within 90 minutes of arrival.

10 · Artifact

Treat the patient, not the monitor

Artifact is electrical noise that isn't from the heart. It can look exactly like ventricular fibrillation. Always check the patient before you call a code.

Common causes

  • Patient moving, brushing teeth, shivering, seizing
  • Loose, dried-out or badly placed electrodes
  • Electrical interference from nearby equipment
  • Sweaty or hairy skin

What to do

  • Look at the patient. Talking and alert means it isn't ventricular fibrillation.
  • Check a pulse and switch to another lead.
  • "Asystole" with a responsive patient usually means a lead fell off.
  • Clean and dry the skin, clip hair, replace electrodes every 24 hours or per policy.
11 · Emergency response

What happens when the rhythm is dangerous

As a student nurse you won't run the code, but you need to know what's coming so you can act fast: call for help, start CPR, bring the crash cart, and anticipate the drugs. Doses follow American Heart Association Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) guidance; always follow your facility's protocol.

First question every time: does the patient have a pulse?

no pulse

Cardiac arrest algorithm. Start CPR now.

pulse, unstable

Low BP, confusion, shock, chest pain, or acute heart failure. Electricity or drugs now.

pulse, stable

Time to get a 12-lead, labs, and provider orders.

Pulseless: shockable VF · pulseless VT

  1. Call a code, start high-quality CPR, attach the defibrillator
  2. Defibrillate (unsynchronized shock)
  3. CPR for 2 minutes, then rhythm check
  4. Epinephrine 1 mg IV every 3–5 minutes
  5. Amiodarone 300 mg IV, then 150 mg if it continues

Pulseless: not shockable asystole · PEA

  1. Start CPR right away. Never shock asystole.
  2. Epinephrine 1 mg IV as soon as possible, then every 3–5 minutes
  3. Check the rhythm every 2 minutes
  4. Look for reversible causes (the H's and T's below)

PEA is pulseless electrical activity: an organized rhythm on the monitor with no pulse.

Bradycardia with a pulse symptomatic

  1. Oxygen if SpO2 is low, IV access, 12-lead
  2. Atropine 1 mg IV, repeat every 3–5 minutes, maximum 3 mg
  3. If atropine fails: transcutaneous pacing, or a dopamine or epinephrine drip

Atropine often won't work in Mobitz type II or third-degree block, so get the pacing pads on early.

Tachycardia with a pulse rate above 150

  1. Unstable: synchronized cardioversion
  2. Stable, narrow, regular (SVT): vagal maneuvers, then adenosine 6 mg rapid IV push with a fast saline flush, then 12 mg if needed
  3. Stable, narrow, irregular (atrial fibrillation): rate control with beta blocker or amiodarone per order
  4. Stable, wide: amiodarone 150 mg IV over 10 minutes, and get expert help

Synchronized vs unsynchronized

Synchronized cardioversion times the shock to the R wave so it doesn't land on the T wave. Use it when there's a pulse. Many machines turn sync off after each shock, so press it again.

Defibrillation is unsynchronized. Use it for ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia, where there is no organized R wave to sync to.

Reversible causes: H's and T's

  • Hypovolemia
  • Hypoxia
  • Hydrogen ion (acidosis)
  • Hypo- or hyperkalemia
  • Hypothermia
  • Tension pneumothorax
  • Tamponade (cardiac)
  • Toxins
  • Thrombosis, pulmonary
  • Thrombosis, coronary

The HR 160 scenario, read with the method

  1. Patient: talking, alert, BP 133/93, SpO2 90%, palpitations, dizzy. Has a pulse, stable but symptomatic.
  2. Rate: 160, tachycardia.
  3. Rhythm and QRS: regular and narrow points to SVT. Irregular and narrow points to atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. Wide points to ventricular tachycardia.
  4. Do: stay, call for help, oxygen, 12-lead, crash cart nearby, potassium and magnesium, SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) for rate or rhythm control orders.
  5. If he becomes unstable: expect synchronized cardioversion.
